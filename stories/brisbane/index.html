Brisbane HSV community story

Brisbane: community before pressure

I joined because I was lonely, not because I was ready for romance. A group of people on a Sunday beach walk showed me that belonging could come first.

First-person community story · Brisbane, Australia · 7 minute read

This story is based on experiences shared within the HSV community. The name and identifying details have been changed to protect privacy.

When I was diagnosed with herpes, everyone told me the medical facts. It was common. It was manageable. Life would continue. They were right, but facts did not sit beside me on Friday nights when I stopped answering invitations. Facts did not explain how to feel normal in a room full of people again.

I’m Josh, from Brisbane. For nearly a year, I avoided dating completely. Friends tried to set me up, and I always found a reason not to go. The idea of attraction had become tangled with disclosure and rejection. I was not ready to be vulnerable with a stranger. What I needed first was to stop feeling like a stranger to myself.

I joined for conversation, not a relationship

My profile said it plainly: “Not ready to date. Just hoping to talk to people who understand.” I expected silence. Instead, a woman named Leah replied, “That’s where a lot of us start.” She invited me to a casual Sunday walk organised by a few local members.

I nearly cancelled that morning. I imagined everyone discussing symptoms in a solemn circle. But when I arrived, two people were arguing about sunscreen, somebody had brought far too many mandarins and a dog called Banjo kept stealing the tennis ball. We walked beside the water, talked about work and complained about the humidity.

Nobody asked me to be brave. They simply made room for me until bravery was no longer required.

HSV came up, but gently. One person had been living with it for fifteen years. Another had been diagnosed three weeks earlier. There was no competition over who had suffered most and no pressure to tell everything. For the first time since my diagnosis, I heard people laugh while speaking about herpes—not because it was trivial, but because it no longer controlled the whole room.

Friendship gave me my future back

I kept joining the walks. Some weeks we met by the river; other weeks we found a quiet stretch of coast outside Brisbane. The group chat became part practical advice, part terrible jokes and part reminders that someone was available when a hard day arrived.

Leah became a close friend. Once, after I admitted that I felt I had fallen behind everyone else, she said, “You’re not late. You’re recovering.” That distinction mattered. I stopped measuring my life against the speed of other people’s relationships.

Months later, I noticed I was talking more openly. I told a mate I trusted about HSV, and his response was simply, “Thanks for trusting me.” I started accepting invitations again. I even went on a date—not because someone pushed me, but because curiosity had finally become stronger than fear.

There is more than one kind of success story

I have not met my forever person yet. For a while, I thought that meant my story was unfinished. Now I know better. Success can be sending the first message. It can be turning up to a walk when you want to hide. It can be finding friends who understand, or waking one morning and realising you no longer feel ashamed.

A supportive herpes community should leave room for all of that. Some people are ready to meet HSV singles in Brisbane; others need information, friendship or a quiet place to begin. Neither path is less hopeful.

If you are standing where I stood, you do not have to force yourself toward romance to prove that you are healing. Start with one conversation. Let somebody understand one honest sentence. Community may not arrive with fireworks. It may look like a borrowed bottle of sunscreen, a shared walk and a group of people waiting while you find your way back.

I joined because I thought I had lost my place in the world. I stayed because people showed me it had been there all along.
